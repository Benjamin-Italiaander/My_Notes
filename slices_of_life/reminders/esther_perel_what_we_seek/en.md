# 1. The paradox inside a safe relationship

According to Esther Perel, love is not the same as desire — and especially in long-term relationships, connection and excitement often clash.

What she is really saying is: we want two things that sometimes work against each other.

1. **Security & being loved**
2. **Excitement & aliveness**

And that is where the friction begins.

---

## 💛 Being loved

According to Perel, “being loved” is about:

* closeness
* predictability
* safety
* familiarity
* being one team together

We want to feel seen. Accepted. Safe.
That brings calm. Connection. Stability.

But…

---

## 🔥 Desire needs something else

According to her, desire actually needs:

* distance
* mystery
* autonomy
* space
* the feeling that the other person has a world of their own

She literally says:

> “Desire needs space.”

When you merge completely, share everything and no longer have a separate identity, desire can fade.
Not because you love each other less.
But because the mystery disappears.

---

## 😴 And that is where boredom comes in

Boredom often doesn't come from a lack of love, but from:

* too much predictability
* too little individual growth
* too little excitement or surprise
* the feeling that everything is fixed

So according to Perel, boredom is not proof that the relationship is “broken” — but a signal that there may be too little **aliveness**.

---

## 💡 Her core idea

A healthy long relationship has to tolerate paradoxes:

* We want to feel safe
* and we want to be surprised
* We want togetherness
* and freedom

That takes conscious maintenance.
Not just “loving each other”, but also staying curious.

---

## 🧠 A beautiful thought of hers

She says desire often returns when you see your partner in their own power — independent, alive, autonomous.

So not when they cling to you.
But when you think:
“Wow, you are your own person.”

---

# 2. Overcoming this paradox

You don't overcome the tension by choosing security *or* desire, but by consciously letting them exist side by side — with structure for connection *and* space for autonomy.

---

## 1️⃣ Stop thinking it has to be solved

According to Esther Perel, this isn't a problem you “fix”.
It is a tension you learn to manage.

Like a campfire:

Too much distance between the logs → the fire goes out.
Too close together, without oxygen → it suffocates.

So it is about **balance, not elimination**.

---

## 2️⃣ Plan security. Let desire breathe.

Very concretely:

### 🔒 Build in active connection

Not just watching Netflix together.

* A weekly moment where you really talk
* Asking each other: “What's going on with you?”
* Small rituals (a Sunday walk, cooking together, etc.)

Security grows through repetition and reliability.

---

### 🔥 But also protect your own space

This is where many couples go wrong.

* Have a hobby your partner isn't part of
* Sometimes go out on your own
* Don't let each other look over your shoulder all the time

Mystery doesn't come from secrets, but from **not sharing everything**.

Your partner doesn't need to own your entire inner world in order to love you.

---

## 3️⃣ See your partner in a different context

Perel says: desire often returns when you see your partner in their own element.

So:

* See them at work
* See them being good at something
* See them having fun without you

That creates “distance” within connection again.
And distance feeds desire.

---

## 4️⃣ Break predictability (without creating drama)

Boredom is often routine + a lack of surprise.

In practice:

* Swap roles
* Go somewhere that makes you slightly uncomfortable
* Do something new together (a course, a trip, a sport)

Novelty triggers dopamine.
Dopamine = curiosity.
Curiosity = aliveness.

---

## 5️⃣ Stop expecting one person to be everything

Modern relationships expect a partner to be:

* best friend
* therapist
* co-parent
* erotic partner
* soulmate

That is a lot.

Sometimes the excitement may also come from other sources:
friends, work, projects, creativity.

That takes pressure off the relationship.

---

## 6️⃣ A mental shift that changes a lot

Instead of:
“Why do I feel less desire?”

Ask:
“When *did* I feel it?”

And look at what was different then:

* more autonomy?
* less stress?
* more playfulness?

---

And perhaps even more important:

Boredom is sometimes not the end of love.
It is an invitation to evolve.
