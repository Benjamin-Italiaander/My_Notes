# Murphyological principles
Murphy's law is the further elaboration and logical continuation of the general theory of entropy.

The theory of entropy says that everything moves towards chaos by itself. The universe as a whole is already moving towards chaos, and that is why things go wrong by themselves:
- Your room gets messy by itself, but it never gets tidy by itself.
- Hot coffee gets cold by itself, but cold coffee does not get hot by itself.
- An ice cube melts by itself, but meltwater does not spontaneously turn back into an ice cube.

## Murphy's law
If something *can* go wrong, it *will* go wrong.
With the invention of the computer, mankind tried for the first time to give lifeless matter a certain degree of intelligence. A fatal decision.
Since the introduction of the computer, we can extend Murphy's law with the following digital corollaries:

---

## Digital corollary 1
Murphy's law is optimised by the computer. However, since modern computers can do several things at once, the second digital corollary follows seamlessly:

## Digital corollary 2
Everything goes wrong at the same time.
With the invention of checksums, correction and backup programs and fault-tolerant systems, mankind gains access to the versatile world of computer science through the third digital corollary:

## Digital corollary 3
It also goes wrong when it actually *can't* go wrong.
Drawing on the experience of the past decades and that of users, programmers and developers, we can now apply Murphy's law and its digital corollaries to the everyday electronic world.

---

## First electronic application of Murphy's law
With computers nothing is unthinkable, let alone impossible — except what is desirable.

## Second electronic application of Murphy's law
In the world of computer science, failures don't pass; they pass into each other.

## Third electronic application of Murphy's law
Computer failures wait patiently for the most unfortunate moment and then strike mercilessly.

## Fourth electronic application of Murphy's law
You can't rely on anything with computers. You can't even rely on the fact that you can't rely on anything.

## Fifth electronic application of Murphy's law
1. You can never prevent a serious failure by causing a small one.
2. At best, a small failure will join a big one to reinforce it.

## Sixth electronic application of Murphy's law
No one can imagine as many failures as actually occur in a computer.

### Conclusions
1. Really serious failures behave like broadcasting directors: they produce as many repeats as possible.
2. Cheap failures don't exist.
3. If a failure seems cheap for once, you haven't yet grasped its true extent.

Since computers and computer science had something to do with mathematics, at least in the beginning, this general introduction to computer Murphyology is not complete without the mathematical proof of Murphy's law.
The reader should bear in mind, however, that mathematics, binary as well as human logic on the one hand, and computer science on the other, have nothing, absolutely nothing, to do with each other.
Except, of course, for the attempt to prove the following.

## Mathematical proof of Murphy's law
The exact mathematical formula for Murphy's law in the field of computer science is as follows:

```
1 + 1 = 2
```

where `=` means: *rarely, if ever*.

---

## Deviation theorem
The difference between digital logic and Murphy's law is that, according to digital logic, you should actually assume that everything always goes wrong in the same way.

---

## Binary translation of the deviation theorem
When zero is particularly large, it is almost as large as a little bit of one.
